# Two Biases, One Knob: Uncorrected n-Step Returns under Reward Noise

*Conference-length version (RLC format). Every number here comes from the results in this folder.*

## Abstract

Uncorrected n-step targets, built from behaviour actions in the replay buffer, are what large-replay value-based agents use. Their fixed point is not \(Q^\ast\). The target blends the behaviour policy into the backup, \(Q \leftarrow (T^\mu)^{n-1} T^\ast Q\), which lies at or below \(Q^\ast\) for an \(\varepsilon\)-greedy \(\mu\), and it still contains Q-learning's max-bias, which enters with weight \(\gamma^n\). On an \(8 \times 8\) slip gridworld with exact \(Q^\ast\) (\(V^\ast = 0.468\)), three reward-noise grids were run, all reported. The grid this paper stands on uses fresh seeds at \(\sigma \in \{0, 0.05, 0.1\}\). Overestimation falls with \(n\) in every noisy cell (Spearman \(-1.00\)), \(n \ge 3\) is pessimistic at \(\sigma = 0\), and the best \(n\) by area under the greedy-return curve rises from 1 at \(\sigma = 0\) to 2–3 at \(\sigma = 0.05\) and to 3 at \(\sigma = 0.1\). In all 10 noisy cells of the second and third grids, that best \(n\) is one of the two horizons whose overestimation is nearest zero. A competence bar written in advance, plain Q at \(\sigma = 0.1\) reaching at least 50% of \(V^\ast\), is missed at 45%. Double Q halves the max-bias at \(n = 1\) and keeps an \(n\)-slope 60–80% as large as plain Q, so the pre-registered double-Q clause fails in every grid. The recorded verdict is INCONCLUSIVE.

## 1 Introduction

Rainbow uses \(n = 3\), R2D2 uses \(n = 5\), and Agent57 and MuZero's reanalyse likewise form n-step targets from stored behaviour trajectories without importance weights (Hessel et al., 2018; Kapturowski et al., 2019). The fixed point of the uncorrected backup is not \(Q^\ast\): the target applies \(n - 1\) steps of the behaviour policy's Bellman operator and a max at the end. The practice persists because it works. Fedus et al. (2020) find that n-step returns are what make large replay buffers help, and Hernandez-Garcia and Sutton (2019) find that importance-weighted and truncated corrections do not help in DQN-style agents. Propagation speed, a reward reaching the start in fewer hops, is shared by corrected and uncorrected n-step. It does not by itself say why the uncorrected target should be the one that helps.

Two biases in the uncorrected target have opposite signs and opposite dependence on \(n\). Blending \(\mu\) pulls the fixed point down as \(n\) and \(\varepsilon\) grow, and the bias is zero at \(n = 1\). Reward noise pulls Q-learning's max up, and that term shrinks with \(n\) because the bootstrapped max is weighted by \(\gamma^n\) and compounds through fewer steps. If the helpful effect of \(n\) is this trade, overestimation should fall with \(n\), the best \(n\) should rise with noise, and the best \(n\) should sit where the two terms cross zero. If double Q removes the max-bias, the \(n\)-dependence should go away with it.

Those claims were pre-registered as a tabular kill test with exact \(V^\ast\), then rewritten twice after the noise scale turned out to be too high for the step size, with the predictions fixed before the fresh-seed replication. On that replication the sign, the ordering, and the zero-crossing hold, and the competence bar is missed by five points. The verdict written for that outcome is INCONCLUSIVE. The double-Q prediction fails in all three grids, and the decomposition says why: the dominant term is the max-bias shrinking with \(n\), which double Q cuts down and does not remove.

**Contributions.** (1) A decomposition of uncorrected n-step overestimation into a pessimistic behaviour-policy term, read off at \(\sigma = 0\), and a max-bias term, the excess over that baseline. (2) Three fully reported \(\sigma\) grids of tabular Q-learning with replay. On fresh seeds the sign and the rise of the best \(n\) with \(\sigma\) replicate, the best \(n\) matches a near-zero crossing in 10/10 noisy cells, and plain Q at \(\sigma = 0.1\) reaches 45% of \(V^\ast\) against a 50% bar, so the verdict is INCONCLUSIVE. (3) Double Q, which was predicted to remove the \(n\)-dependence: it halves the \(n = 1\) max-bias (\(+0.062\) against \(+0.132\) at \(\sigma = 0.1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\)) and its slope on \(\log n\) stays 60–80% of plain Q's. K3 fails in every grid.

## 2 Related work

Uncorrected multi-step targets in deep agents are surveyed by the systems that shipped them (Hessel et al., 2018; Kapturowski et al., 2019) and by the replay ablation of Fedus et al. (2020). The correction literature includes Retrace (Munos et al., 2016), Tree-backup (Precup, Sutton, and Singh, 2000), and \(Q(\sigma)\) (De Asis et al., 2018). Hernandez-Garcia and Sutton (2019) compare corrections in DQN-style agents and find they do not help. Max-bias and the double estimator are Thrun and Schwartz (1993), van Hasselt (2010), and van Hasselt, Guez, and Silver (2016); ensemble and minimax variants are Lan et al. (2020) and Chen et al. (2021). None of those treats \(n\) as the knob on the max-bias. Pessimism from evaluating toward the behaviour policy is used on purpose in offline RL; Kostrikov, Nair, and Levine (2022) use expectile regression partly for that reason. The online n-step update has the same blending, here as a side effect of reading actions out of the buffer, and the experiments measure its size at \(\sigma = 0\).

## 3 The accounting

An uncorrected n-step target along a stored behaviour trajectory is \(G = \sum_{k < n} \gamma^k r_{t+k} + \gamma^n \max_a \bar Q(s_{t+n}, a)\). In expectation over \(\mu\)'s actions the first \(n - 1\) steps apply the behaviour Bellman operator and the last step applies the optimality operator: \(Q \leftarrow (T^\mu)^{n-1} T^\ast \bar Q\). The fixed point \(Q_n^\mu\) satisfies \(Q_n^\mu \le Q^\ast\), with equality when \(\mu\) is greedy along the trajectory. For \(\varepsilon\)-greedy \(\mu\) the gap at the start grows with \(n\) and with \(\varepsilon\), and it is zero at \(n = 1\). Write \(\mathrm{pess}(n, \varepsilon) \le 0\) for the overestimation this term contributes. At finite \(n\) the measured \(\sigma = 0\) overestimation need not be negative at \(n = 1\), where a small positive residual remains; the prediction that was tested is pessimism for \(n \ge 3\) at \(\sigma = 0\).

Under zero-mean reward noise of scale \(\sigma\), \(\max_a \bar Q\) of noisy action-values is biased high (Thrun and Schwartz, 1993). In the n-step target that bias is multiplied by \(\gamma^n\) and compounds over fewer bootstraps. Write \(\mathrm{maxbias}(n, \sigma)\) for the excess of overestimation over the \(\sigma = 0\) value at the same \(n\). The tested predictions are: total overestimation falls with \(n\) whenever \(\sigma > 0\), and is at most zero for \(n \ge 3\) at \(\sigma = 0\); the \(n\) that maximises return rises with \(\sigma\); the best \(n\) is where the sum of the two terms is nearest zero. A further pre-registered prediction, that double Q removes the max-bias and therefore removes the dependence of overestimation on \(n\), is stated in Section 4 and fails.

The decomposition used in the results defines \(\mathrm{pess}(n)\) as measured overestimation at \(\sigma = 0\), and \(\mathrm{maxbias}(n, \sigma)\) as overestimation at that \(\sigma\) minus \(\mathrm{pess}(n)\). The max-bias column can be slightly negative at large \(n\). That is what the subtraction produces on these runs; it is reported as measured.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setup

The environment is an \(8 \times 8\) gridworld with slip 0.1, discount \(\gamma = 0.95\), reward 1 on entering the absorbing goal, and zero-mean Gaussian reward noise of standard deviation \(\sigma\) on every step. Noise does not change \(Q^\ast\). Episodes are capped at 100. \(V^\ast(s_0) = 0.468\). The agent is tabular Q-learning with a FIFO replay buffer of 5000, batch 32, step size \(\alpha = 0.1\) per sampled update, and a target table refreshed every 100 updates. Targets are uncorrected n-step returns for \(n \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 10\}\). Behaviour is \(\varepsilon\)-greedy at constant \(\varepsilon \in \{0.1, 0.3\}\), with exploring starts. Double Q uses the online table for the argmax and the target table for the evaluation. Each run is 150k steps, with 4 seeds. Overestimation is \(\sum_s d^{\pi_g}(s)\,(\max_a Q(s, a) - V^\ast(s))\), where \(d^{\pi_g}\) is the greedy policy's discounted state visitation from the start. Greedy return is the exact \(V^{\pi_g}(s_0)\). Both are averaged over the last five checkpoints. The best \(n\) in a cell is the one with the highest area under the greedy-return curve.

### 4.2 Three grids, and the rule that names the verdict

The first grid is \(\sigma \in \{0, 0.5, 1.5\}\), seeds 0–3. K1 holds: Spearman of overestimation on \(n\) is \(-1.00\) in all four noisy cells, and every \(n \ge 3\) is pessimistic at \(\sigma = 0\). K2 holds by the letter: the best \(n\) by area under the curve is 1 at \(\sigma = 0\) and 10 at \(\sigma = 0.5\) and at \(\sigma = 1.5\), for both \(\varepsilon\). K3 fails: the slope of overestimation on \(\log n\) is \(-1.879\) for plain Q and \(-1.194\) for double Q, and the double-Q slope is not less than half the plain-Q slope in magnitude. K2 on this grid does not compare working agents. At \(\sigma > 0\) the max-bias is 2–9, which is 5–20× \(V^\ast = 0.468\), greedy return is about 0.001, and no agent exceeded 0.4% of \(V^\ast\). The original rule would say SUPPORTED. The follow-up rule, written before the later grids, retires that label because the best \(n\) there ranks failed agents.

The second grid, pre-registered with a working-agent condition, is \(\sigma \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}\), seeds 0–3. K1 and K2 pass again. The best \(n\) for plain Q goes \(1\), then \(3\), then \(5\)–\(10\), then \(10\) as \(\sigma\) goes \(0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\). K3 fails again. The working-agent condition required plain Q at \(\sigma_{\max} = 0.3\) to reach at least 50% of \(V^\ast\) for at least one \(n\). The best return there is 0.7% of \(V^\ast\). At \(\sigma = 0.1\) on these seeds the best plain-Q final return is 55% of \(V^\ast\) and the best \(n\) is 3. By the follow-up rule this grid is not SUPPORTED.

The third grid was pre-registered from the second, at a noise level where agents had reached \(V^\ast\) in that earlier seed set: \(\sigma \in \{0, 0.05, 0.1\}\), fresh seeds 4–7. Four predictions were fixed in advance. P1: K1's sign pattern, overestimation falling with \(n\) at \(\sigma = 0.05\) and \(0.1\), and \(n \ge 3\) pessimistic at \(\sigma = 0\). P2: the best \(n\) by area under the curve is larger at \(\sigma = 0.1\) than at \(\sigma = 0\) for both \(\varepsilon\), and the best \(n\) at \(\sigma = 0.05\) lies between them, inclusive. P3: plain Q at \(\sigma = 0.1\) reaches at least 50% of \(V^\ast\) for at least one \(n\). P4, the mechanism check: at \(\sigma = 0.1\) the best \(n\) is one of the two horizons whose overestimation is nearest zero. The recorded rule is SUPPORTED if P1–P3 hold, with P4 reported alongside, and INCONCLUSIVE if P2 fails only in the ordering at \(\sigma = 0.05\). P3's failure is not a kill clause. P1, P2, and P4 hold, and P3 fails at 45% of \(V^\ast\). The recorded verdict is INCONCLUSIVE.

### 4.4 Where the sum crosses zero

Splitting the fresh-seed table at \(\sigma = 0.1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) gives \(\mathrm{maxbias} = +0.122, +0.017, +0.016, -0.021, -0.068\) and \(\mathrm{pess} = +0.010, -0.002, -0.007, -0.011, -0.018\) for \(n = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10\). The max-bias falls 7× from \(n = 1\) to \(n = 2\) and is no longer a positive term at \(n = 3\). Across the noisy cells the same drop from \(n = 1\) to \(n = 2\) is 5–10×, and the max-bias is near zero by \(n = 3\) for \(\sigma \le 0.1\). At \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) the pessimistic term goes from \(+0.010\) at \(n = 1\) to \(-0.002\) at \(n = 2\) and to \(-0.018\) at \(n = 10\); at \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) and \(\sigma = 0\) it is \(-0.058\) at \(n = 10\). The fall of total overestimation with \(n\) is the max-bias shrinking. The pessimistic term is the smaller, opposite-signed correction, and it is what pushes the sum through zero.

P4 scores the weak form of cancellation: the best \(n\) by area under the curve is one of the two \(n\) whose total overestimation is nearest zero. On plain Q, pooling the second and third grids and excluding the first grid because no agent learned there, the match is 10/10. Under a chance rate of \(2/5\) per cell, \(0.4^{10} = 10^{-4}\).

### 4.5 Double Q

K3 asked that the slope of overestimation on \(\log n\), averaged over noisy cells, be less than half as large in magnitude for double Q as for plain Q. On the first grid the slopes are \(-1.879\) and \(-1.194\). On all three grids the double-Q slope is 60–80% of plain Q's. K3 fails three times.

On the fresh seeds, double Q's max-bias at \(n = 1\) is \(+0.058\) at \(\sigma = 0.1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), against plain Q's \(+0.122\) in the decomposition above, and the recorded totals at \(n = 1\) are \(+0.062\) against \(+0.132\). At \(\sigma = 0.05\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the \(n = 1\) totals are \(+0.020\) against \(+0.037\). The \(\sigma = 0\) rows of plain and double Q agree to \(\pm 0.005\): the pessimistic term is shared. Double Q's best \(n\) at \(\sigma = 0.1\) is 5 at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) (final returns \(35\%, 53\%, 51\%, 59\%, 27\%\) of \(V^\ast\) across \(n = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10\)) and 3 at \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) (final returns \(19\%, 40\%, 24\%, 28\%, 17\%\)). The premise of K3, that this double estimator removes the max-bias, is false in this design. It cuts the \(n = 1\) bias by about half, and \(n > 1\) still reduces what remains. The implementation is the standard one-target-table form: online argmax, target evaluation.

## 5 Discussion and limitations

The fresh-seed grid is the one the rule tells us to stand on, and the rule's verdict is INCONCLUSIVE. P1 and P2 replicated on seeds 4–7: overestimation is monotone in \(n\) at Spearman \(-1.00\) in every noisy cell, \(n \ge 3\) is pessimistic with no reward noise, and the best \(n\) moves from 1 to 2–3 to 3 as \(\sigma\) moves from 0 to 0.05 to 0.1. P4 holds in a stronger pool than the replication alone, 10/10 noisy cells, with the caveat that the second grid's \(\sigma = 0.2\) and \(\sigma = 0.3\) cells are failing policies. P3 was a competence bar of 50% of \(V^\ast\) at \(\sigma = 0.1\), missed at 45%, after the previous seeds had reached 55%. Five points on four seeds is enough to refuse the SUPPORTED label, which is what the rule does. It is not evidence against the sign or the ordering, which are the clauses that passed.

The decomposition changes the emphasis of the original hypothesis. Both terms are present and they have opposite signs at the \(n\) where the total crosses zero. The large movement between \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\) is the max-bias, from \(+0.122\) to \(+0.017\) at \(\sigma = 0.1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), while pessimism over the whole range \(n = 1\) to \(n = 10\) is \(+0.010\) to \(-0.018\) at that \(\varepsilon\). Uncorrected n-step helps under this noise because a longer target leans less on the bootstrapped max. It hurts with the noise turned off because the behaviour operator is still in the target: 11–13% of final return at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and 22–23% at \(\varepsilon = 0.3\), at \(n = 10\), for both estimators. The \(\varepsilon\)-dependence of that cost is visible at \(\sigma = 0\) in the table, \(-0.018\) against \(-0.058\) at \(n = 10\).

Double Q is a partial substitute for the max-bias term and shares the pessimistic term, so the two knobs do not cancel. Anyone choosing \(n\) from the double-Q column on these seeds still prefers \(n = 3\) or \(n = 5\) at \(\sigma = 0.1\).

The noise scale was set three times. The first two top-end values, \(\sigma = 1.5\) and \(\sigma = 0.3\), produce agents at 0.4% and 0.7% of \(V^\ast\). With \(\alpha = 0.1\), a payoff of 1, and episodes of 100 steps, the agent has already failed outright by \(\sigma = 0.2\), where the reward standard deviation per step is about 0.5. All three grids are in the results. Transition slip was fixed at 0.1 and was not swept; it is a second source of max-bias. One step size, one gridworld, and four seeds per cell mean the 45% against 55% gap between seed sets is within the design. Area under a 150k-step curve rewards early improvement, and early improvement is also where a shorter backup propagates faster. A corrected-versus-uncorrected comparison at matched \(n\) was not part of this run. MinAtar experiments were not run.

## 6 Conclusion

Uncorrected n-step returns on this gridworld mix a pessimistic behaviour-policy bias, growing with \(n\) and \(\varepsilon\), with Q-learning's max-bias, which falls 5–10× between \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\). On fresh seeds, overestimation falls with \(n\) at Spearman \(-1.00\), the best \(n\) by area under the curve goes from 1 at \(\sigma = 0\) to 2–3 at \(\sigma = 0.05\) and to 3 at \(\sigma = 0.1\), and that \(n\) is one of the two nearest a zero crossing in all 10 noisy cells of the second and third grids. The same fresh seeds reach 45% of \(V^\ast\) where the pre-registered bar was 50%, after a previous seed set had reached 55%. Double Q halves the max-bias and does not remove the \(n\)-dependence, in any of the three grids. The recorded verdict is INCONCLUSIVE.
